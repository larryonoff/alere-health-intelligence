# Evidence Labels

Read this whenever stating how well established a material health claim is.
These labels describe **certainty of evidence**, not recommendation strength.
A guideline can strongly recommend an action despite low certainty, or make a
conditional recommendation despite high certainty. When they differ, state both.

## Labels

- `strong` — current applicable guidance from a recognized authority or
  consistent human randomized evidence, for the population being asked about.
  Say: “This is well established,” and name the authority.
- `moderate` — human trials point in the same direction, but are limited by
  size, duration, setting, or lack of guideline adoption. Say: “Trials support
  this, though the evidence base is limited.”
- `weak` — pilot, uncontrolled, or observational evidence without adequate
  trial confirmation, or a guideline that explicitly rates its own
  recommendation as low-quality, whatever the issuing body's stature.
  Say: “This may help, but is not established.”
- `disputed` — reputable sources or trials conflict. State who disagrees and
  about what.
- `insufficient` — no usable human evidence, or only mechanistic, animal, or
  in-vitro work. Say plainly that reliable human evidence is absent.

## Runtime rules

1. Label each claim, not the whole topic.
2. Assess the relevant population and setting.
3. Do not infer certainty from source type alone.
4. Distinguish association from causation and surrogate outcomes from health
   outcomes.
5. A plausible mechanism does not establish an effect.
6. Never upgrade a label to satisfy the user or downgrade it merely to sound
   cautious.
7. Use the reviewed entry's label. If no entry exists, disclose the corpus gap
   and verify externally when that path is available.
