# Clinician-Authored Popular Books — Editorial Research Card

Status: development reference only  
Reviewed: 2026-07-25  
Review cycle: annual

This card covers popular books written by licensed clinicians who advocate a
plant-based or plant-forward diet. It is not a source of claims for user
responses. Nothing here is promotable to `evidence/` in its current form.

## Why this class of source needs its own card

These books are written by qualified clinicians, are widely read, and are
frequently the reason a user arrives with a specific belief. That combination —
real credentials, real influence, no peer review — is exactly the case the
promotion checklist's independence step exists for.

Under `editorial-promotion-checklist.md` §2 they classify as **educational
summary** with a commercial interest, one step above testimonial and well below
a guideline. A physician author raises the prior that a claim is defensible; it
does not make the book a citable authority. The claim still has to be traced to
the underlying study or guideline and labeled on its own merits.

Three failure modes are specific to this class:

- **Credential transfer.** A cardiologist writing about cardiology is still
  writing a trade book, not a guideline. The MD attaches to the author, not to
  each sentence.
- **Uniform confidence.** Trade books are edited for readability, so
  well-established claims and speculative ones are written in the same
  confident voice. The book's own tone carries no strength information.
- **Selective citation.** Advocacy books cite supporting literature and rarely
  the contradicting body. A citation appearing in the book is not evidence that
  the weight of evidence points that way.

## Shared handling rules

1. Never cite a book as the source for a health claim. Locate the underlying
   guideline, review, or trial and cite that.
2. If the underlying source cannot be located, the claim does not enter the
   corpus and is not stated to the user as established.
3. When a user names one of these books, say what the reviewed corpus shows on
   the same question, and identify the book as a clinician's popular book
   rather than a guideline. Do not disparage the author.
4. Where a book's position is stronger than the reviewed evidence — the common
   case — report the corpus label, not the book's confidence.
5. Do not copy book text, framing devices, branded programs, meal plans, or
   recipes. Copyright applies and the project may become commercial.
6. Points 1–5 apply equally when the book's conclusion agrees with the corpus.
   Agreement is not verification.

## The Plant-Based Solution — Joel Kahn, MD

Cardiologist; plant-based advocacy for cardiovascular prevention.

Overlaps `evidence/conditions/elevated-ldl.md`,
`evidence/conditions/hypertension.md`, and
`evidence/longevity/dietary-patterns.md`.

Where the reviewed corpus is likely to agree, after independent sourcing:
plant-forward dietary patterns, fibre intake, and replacement of saturated fat
have real supporting evidence and are already represented in the corpus from
guideline sources.

Where caution applies:

- **Reversal framing.** Cardiac trade books commonly invoke atherosclerosis
  reversal. The underlying trials are small, unblinded, intensive-lifestyle
  studies with surrogate imaging endpoints. This is a disease claim under
  `editorial-promotion-checklist.md` §4 and cannot be repeated in any form.
- **Single-pattern superiority.** A claim that one dietary pattern beats all
  others requires head-to-head outcome evidence that does not exist. The corpus
  position on competing patterns lives in `evidence/longevity/dietary-patterns.md`.
- **Supplement and testing suggestions.** Out of scope regardless of the source
  — see `safety-and-context.md`.
- **Author commercial interests.** Clinics, products, and programs linked to the
  author are a conflict to record, per checklist §3.

Verification queue, if a real user need appears: locate the AHA/ACC and ESC
positions on dietary pattern and LDL, and the current systematic reviews of
plant-based diets and cardiovascular outcomes. Cite those, never the book.

## The Plant Power Doctor — Gemma Newman, MD

General practitioner; plant-based nutrition across general and women's health.

Overlaps the same conditions entries plus `evidence/nutrients/fiber.md`.

The general-practice framing — small sustainable changes, whole-diet quality
over single nutrients — is directionally consistent with the corpus and with
`SKILL.md`'s decision rules. That consistency is a reason to check the sources,
not a reason to skip checking them.

Where caution applies:

- **Women's-health claims.** The backlog already flags cycle-synchronized
  nutrition as unsupported. Any hormonal, menstrual, menopause, or fertility
  claim needs female-specific systematic reviews or consensus statements before
  it is stated at any strength above `insufficient`.
- **Condition-specific dietary claims** beyond the reviewed conditions require
  their own sourcing and their own entry.
- **Elimination framing.** Any suggestion to drop a food group without a
  diagnosed indication routes to `disordered-eating-safety.md`, which takes
  precedence over the nutrition question.

## A Parent's Guide to Intuitive Eating — Yami Cazorla-Lancaster, DO

Paediatrician; feeding children without restriction, weight talk, or food
moralizing.

This one is different from the other two, and worth taking seriously as an
editorial input rather than only as a claim risk.

**Relevance.** Its core positions — the division of responsibility in feeding,
no food moralizing, no weight talk with children, no restriction as a behaviour
tool — are not fringe advocacy. They align with established paediatric feeding
practice and with the principles already encoded in
`disordered-eating-safety.md`, which prohibits food ranking, restriction
mechanics, and body comment for the same reasons.

**Scope problem.** Paediatric and family nutrition is currently out of scope.
`safety-and-context.md` routes children's questions to qualified care, and the
backlog lists childhood nutrition guidelines under future additions. This book
does not change that scope, and it is not a substitute for the paediatric
guidelines that would be needed to open it.

**Editorial use.** The book is useful as evidence that a real user need exists
in this area — a parent with a metabolic marker asking what to feed their
children is a plausible and currently unserved case. It is not useful as the
source that would serve it.

If paediatric scope is ever opened, the sourcing must come from paediatric
authorities — AAP, WHO/UNICEF child feeding guidance, ESPGHAN, and the
Satter Institute's published work on the division of responsibility — not from
this book. Intuitive-eating framing brought into a product also needs its own
safety review: the concept is protective in children and is also marketed
widely, and the two uses are not interchangeable.

Until then: `safety-and-context.md` continues to govern anything involving
children.

## Editorial decision

Retain all three as named limited sources in `source-catalog.md` for the "user
asked about this book" case. Do not promote any claim from them into `evidence/`
without independent authoritative sourcing and independent wording.
