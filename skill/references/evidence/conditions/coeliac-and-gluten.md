# Coeliac disease, wheat allergy, and gluten sensitivity

Read this whenever gluten comes up: a user considering cutting it, reporting
symptoms they attribute to it, quoting advice to eliminate it, or asking whether
they should be tested.

**The single most consequential rule in this file:** testing must happen *before*
gluten is removed. Self-elimination is not a diagnostic test — it destroys the
evidence a diagnosis depends on. If the user is about to eliminate gluten, say
this first, before anything else about diet.

## Claims

### Three distinct conditions, with different mechanisms and different stakes

**Strength: strong.**

Gluten-related disorders are not one thing. The clinically accepted split is
autoimmune (coeliac disease), IgE-mediated (wheat allergy), and neither
(non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, increasingly called non-coeliac *wheat*
sensitivity because fructans and amylase-trypsin inhibitors — not gluten — may be
the actual trigger).

- Dale HF, Biesiekierski JR, Lied GA. Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity and the
  spectrum of gluten-related disorders: an updated overview. *Nutrition Research
  Reviews* 2018;32(1):28–37.
  DOI: [10.1017/S095442241800015X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095442241800015X).

The fuller classification includes two further gluten-dependent autoimmune
entities that a three-bucket model omits: **dermatitis herpetiformis** (the
cutaneous manifestation of coeliac disease) and **gluten ataxia**. Both are real
and both resolve or improve on a gluten-free diet — but only after diagnosis.

### Testing requires gluten still in the diet. Elimination first is the error.

**Strength: strong.**

Serology and biopsy both lose accuracy once gluten is withdrawn: antibodies fall
within weeks and villous architecture heals over months. The diagnostic window
closes.

- tTG-IgA is the first-line serological test, with **sensitivity 63–93% and
  specificity 96–100%** in patients consuming gluten. Serology and endoscopy are
  not accurate for diagnosis in someone already gluten-free. Four or more duodenal
  biopsy sites are recommended, because the lesion can be patchy.
  Rubio-Tapia A, Hill ID, Semrad C, et al. American College of Gastroenterology
  guidelines update: diagnosis and management of celiac disease. *Am J
  Gastroenterol* 2023;118(1):59–76.
  DOI: [10.14309/ajg.0000000000002075](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000002075).
  Open summary:
  <https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2024/0100/practice-guidelines-celiac-disease.html>
- NICE NG20 advises eating gluten in more than one meal every day for **at least
  six weeks before testing**, and not starting a gluten-free diet until diagnosis
  is confirmed by a specialist — even if serology is positive.
  <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng20/chapter/recommendations>
  (Recommendation wording taken from indexed summaries; the NICE site blocks
  automated retrieval, so verify against the live page before quoting numbers.)

Recovering a diagnosis after self-elimination requires a deliberate gluten
challenge — commonly described as roughly three slices of wheat bread daily for
one to three months — which reintroduces symptoms and which many people decline or
cannot tolerate.

Two claims in circulation are wrong and worth naming plainly:

- **"Serology is only about 30% accurate."** Not supported by any guideline. The
  figures above are the published performance. Accuracy collapses *after* gluten
  withdrawal, which is a fact about test conditions, not about the assay.
- **"A month without gluten is 100% diagnostic — if you feel better, it harms
  you."** Symptom improvement does not distinguish coeliac disease from wheat
  allergy, from NCGS, from a FODMAP response, or from placebo. It is the one
  approach that forfeits the diagnosis.

### Coeliac disease affects roughly 1%, and most cases are undiagnosed

**Strength: strong** for prevalence.

Pooled **seroprevalence 1.4% (95% CI 1.1–1.7)** across 275,818 people, and
**biopsy-confirmed 0.7% (95% CI 0.5–0.9)** across 138,792. Europe and Oceania
0.8%; higher in children (0.9%) than adults (0.5%); women 0.6% versus men 0.4%.

- Singh P, Arora A, Strand TA, et al. Global prevalence of celiac disease:
  systematic review and meta-analysis. *Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol*
  2018;16(6):823–836.
  DOI: [10.1016/j.cgh.2017.06.037](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cgh.2017.06.037).

That a large share of cases go undiagnosed is well accepted; specific percentages
in circulation come from advocacy-organisation ranges rather than meta-analysis.
State the direction, not a precise figure.

### Wheat allergy is far rarer than 1%

**Strength: moderate.**

Across 56 studies: self-reported 0.63%, physician-diagnosed 0.70%, skin-prick
positive 0.22%, sIgE positive 0.97%, but **oral food challenge–confirmed 0.04%
(95% CI 0–0.16)**.

- Liu Z, Wu Y, Meng J, et al. A meta-analysis of the prevalence of wheat allergy
  worldwide. *Nutrients* 2023;15(7):1564.
  DOI: [10.3390/nu15071564](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15071564).

Self-report inflates food-allergy prevalence substantially. Cite the
challenge-confirmed figure and say which kind of number it is.

### NCGS has no biomarker, and blinded challenge confirms it in a minority

**Strength: moderate.**

NCGS is a diagnosis of exclusion: coeliac disease and wheat allergy must be ruled
out first, ideally followed by double-blind placebo-controlled challenge.

- Catassi C, Elli L, Bonaz B, et al. Diagnosis of non-celiac gluten sensitivity
  (NCGS): the Salerno experts' criteria. *Nutrients* 2015;7(6):4966–4977.
  DOI: [10.3390/nu7064966](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu7064966).

Population estimates span roughly 0.6–13% and the true prevalence is unknown;
self-reported figures around 10% are not confirmed-disease figures. Where blinded
rechallenge has been done, gluten-specific effects are confirmed in only a
minority, and fructan reduction often explains the improvement. The gap between
self-report and confirmation runs *downward*, not upward — claims that the real
number is far higher than survey estimates invert this.

"We cannot diagnose gluten sensitivity in a laboratory" is true for NCGS
specifically. It is false for coeliac disease, and generalising it across the
three conditions is how people end up untested.

### Coeliac red flags belong to coeliac disease, not to self-diagnosed sensitivity

**Strength: moderate.**

Iron-deficiency anaemia, autoimmune thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, and
unexplained neurological symptoms are genuine coeliac associations — coeliac
disease is found in roughly 3–5% of iron-deficiency anaemia cases, and thyroid
disease is substantially more common in coeliac patients. These are shared-HLA
co-occurrences, not evidence that gluten causes thyroid disease in the general
population, and a gluten-free diet has not been shown to improve thyroid or
diabetes outcomes in people without coeliac disease.

The practical consequence is the reason this claim is here: attaching these red
flags to "gluten sensitivity" steers exactly the people who most need coeliac
testing toward self-elimination instead.

- Rubio-Tapia A et al. American College of Gastroenterology Guidelines Update:
  Diagnosis and Management of Celiac Disease. *Am J Gastroenterol* 2023.
  DOI: [10.14309/ajg.0000000000002075](https://doi.org/10.14309/ajg.0000000000002075).

### For people without coeliac disease or wheat allergy, gluten avoidance is not indicated

**Strength: moderate.**

Whole grains containing gluten — wheat, rye, barley — are among the exposures
associated with lower cardiovascular and mortality risk; see `../nutrients/fiber.md`.
"Gluten-free" is not a synonym for "wholegrain": commercial gluten-free products
are frequently refined, lower in fibre, and higher in sugar and fat.

In two large prospective cohorts, gluten intake was not associated with coronary
heart disease, and after adjustment for refined-grain intake higher gluten intake
was associated with *lower* risk. The authors concluded that promotion of
gluten-free diets among people without coeliac disease should not be encouraged.

- Lebwohl B, Cao Y, Zong G, et al. Long term gluten consumption in adults without
  celiac disease and risk of coronary heart disease: prospective cohort study.
  *BMJ* 2017;357:j1892.
  DOI: [10.1136/bmj.j1892](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j1892).

"No need to exclude gluten if you feel fine" is correct. "But reduce it anyway,
gluten-free grains are healthier" does not follow and is not supported.

## What to say

1. **If the user has not yet eliminated gluten:** testing comes first, on a
   gluten-containing diet. This is the priority message.
2. **If they have already eliminated it:** say plainly that testing is now
   compromised and that a gluten challenge under clinical supervision is what
   restores the option. Do not design the challenge.
3. **If they report a coeliac red flag** — anaemia, thyroid disease, unexplained
   neurological symptoms, a first-degree relative with coeliac disease — route to a
   clinician for testing rather than discussing diet.
4. Distinguish the three conditions by name when the user conflates them, and say
   which one their question actually concerns.
5. Never state or imply that symptom improvement on elimination establishes a
   diagnosis.

## Boundary

- Do not diagnose, interpret serology results, or advise on biopsy.
- Do not design or schedule a gluten challenge — supervision is clinical.
- Do not recommend gluten elimination as a trial, a diagnostic step, or a
  general-health measure. Elimination requests route per
  `../../disordered-eating-safety.md`, which takes precedence over the nutrition
  question.
- A confirmed coeliac diagnosis makes a strict lifelong gluten-free diet medically
  indicated; that is dietitian and specialist territory, not general advice. Route
  per `../../safety-and-context.md`.
- Do not attribute an autoimmune diagnosis to gluten for an individual.
