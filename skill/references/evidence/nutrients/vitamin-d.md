# Vitamin D

Read this for questions about vitamin D: whether diet matters, sun exposure, dosing,
sunscreen, and claims that supplementation is unnecessary or that high doses are
toxic.

Latitude governs the answer more than anything else here. At 55–60°N — Moscow,
St Petersburg, most of the Nordic and Baltic region, northern Poland — cutaneous
synthesis effectively stops for several months a year. Advice built around "get
fifteen minutes of sun" does not work for that period, and telling such an audience
that vitamin D is unrelated to diet leaves them with no route at all.

## Claims

### Reference values

**Strength: strong.**

EFSA sets an **adequate intake of 15 µg/day** for adults and a **tolerable upper
intake level of 100 µg/day**.

- EFSA NDA Panel. Scientific opinion on the tolerable upper intake level for
  vitamin D. *EFSA Journal* 2023;21(8):8145.
  DOI: [10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8145](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8145).

NNR2023 sets a recommended intake of **10 µg/day**, rising to **20 µg/day for people
aged 75 and over and for those with little or no sun exposure**, and states that at
Nordic and Baltic latitudes (54–71°N) solar radiation is insufficient for cutaneous
synthesis during part of the year. It names vegans as a risk group unless consuming
supplements or fortified foods.

Note the two bodies differ (15 versus 10 µg/day). This is a legitimate regional
difference in reference-value derivation, not an error — see
`../guidelines/conflicting-guidance.md`.

### Diet and fortification measurably affect status

**Strength: strong.**

The claim that vitamin D is unrelated to nutrition is contradicted by direct
measurement. In EPIC-Oxford, intake and plasma 25(OH)D tracked degree of animal-food
exclusion: meat eaters **3.1 µg/day** intake and **77.0 nmol/L** plasma, vegans
**0.7 µg/day** and **55.8 nmol/L**.

- Crowe FL, Steur M, Allen NE, et al. Plasma concentrations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D in
  meat eaters, fish eaters, vegetarians and vegans. *Public Health Nutrition*
  2011;14(2):340–346.
  DOI: [10.1017/S1368980010002454](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980010002454).

Fortification works, and the effect size is substantial: a meta-analysis of 23 RCTs
found vitamin D–fortified foods raised circulating 25(OH)D by
**25.4 nmol/L (95% CI 19.5–31.3)**.

- Nikooyeh B, Neyestani TR. The effects of vitamin D-fortified foods on circulating
  25(OH)D concentrations in adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *British
  Journal of Nutrition* 2022;127(12):1821–1838.
  DOI: [10.1017/S0007114521002816](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114521002816).

A qualification worth stating: in sunny settings with high supplement use, dietary
differences by vegetarian status have been less apparent. That bounds the magnitude,
not the existence, of the dietary contribution — and it is the opposite of the
situation at high latitude.

### Sun exposure is seasonal, and cannot be relied on in winter at high latitude

**Strength: strong.**

Cutaneous synthesis requires UVB, whose intensity falls below the threshold for
production at high latitude in winter regardless of time spent outdoors. This is the
"vitamin D winter." Skin pigmentation, age, cloud cover, clothing, and time of day
all reduce synthesis further.

Practical consequence: at 55–60°N a supplement or fortified food is the realistic
route for several months a year. Advising sun exposure alone for that period is not
actionable.

### Sunscreen is not the reason people are deficient

**Strength: moderate.**

Laboratory sun-protection factors are derived from application rates far heavier than
real-world use, and field studies consistently find that people using sunscreen do
not develop lower vitamin D status — in part because sunscreen use accompanies more
time outdoors. The advice to leave children unprotected in the sun to raise vitamin D
trades a documented skin-cancer risk against a deficiency that a supplement resolves
directly. Do not repeat it.

### High-dose supplementation is not a general recommendation

**Strength: moderate.**

The EFSA upper limit is 100 µg/day (4000 IU). Routine intakes at that level are not
recommended for the general population, and higher doses have not shown outcome
benefit in large trials. Toxicity — hypercalcaemia — occurs at sustained intakes well
above the upper limit, so the framing that 5000 IU is inherently "toxic" is not
accurate either; it is above what guidance supports without being an acute poison.

State the reference values and the upper limit. Do not select a dose for the
individual: appropriate dosing depends on baseline status, body weight, season,
latitude, and medication, and correcting a measured deficiency is clinical work.

## What to say

1. Ask or infer latitude and season before answering "how much sun is enough."
2. At high latitude in winter, name a supplement or fortified foods as the route,
   not sun exposure.
3. Give the EFSA and NNR2023 figures and note that they differ; do not average them
   or prescribe a personal dose.
4. If the user reports a measured 25(OH)D result or symptoms, route to a clinician
   rather than interpreting it.
5. Do not endorse skipping sun protection for children or adults as a vitamin D
   strategy.

## Boundary

- Do not interpret 25(OH)D results or diagnose deficiency or insufficiency.
- Do not recommend a treatment dose, a loading dose, or an injection schedule.
- Do not advise on vitamin D in pregnancy, infancy, or childhood beyond noting that
  supplementation is commonly recommended and is clinician-directed. Route per
  `../../safety-and-context.md`.
- Do not advise on vitamin D alongside calcium supplements, thiazides, or in
  sarcoidosis, hyperparathyroidism, or chronic kidney disease — hypercalcaemia risk
  is clinical territory.
- Do not present vitamin D as treatment for autoimmune disease, depression,
  respiratory infection, or cancer.
