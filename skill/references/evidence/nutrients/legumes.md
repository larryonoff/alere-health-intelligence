# Legumes and pulses

Read this for questions about beans, lentils, chickpeas, peas, and soy — how much,
how often, whether soaking matters, and whether "antinutrients" are a reason to
avoid them.

Two opposite errors are common here. One treats legumes as a daily obligation with
a therapeutic effect the evidence does not carry. The other treats phytate and
lectins as a reason to avoid legumes, which the outcome data contradict. A third,
narrower error is more consequential than either: dismissing soaking as merely a
way to cook faster.

## Claims

### Legumes as a regular part of the diet are associated with modest benefit

**Strength: moderate.**

A meta-analysis of 14 studies (11 cohorts, ~367,000 people, 18,475 cardiovascular
cases) found highest versus lowest legume intake associated with lower CVD and
coronary heart disease risk, **RR 0.90 (95% CI 0.84–0.97)**; the association for
stroke was null. Note the exposure: the highest category was estimated at roughly
**3–4 servings per week**, not daily intake, and no dose-response could be
determined.

- Marventano S, Izquierdo Pulido M, Sánchez-González C, et al. Legume consumption
  and CVD risk: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Public Health Nutrition*
  2017;20(2):245–254.
  DOI: [10.1017/S1368980016002299](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980016002299).

For mortality, a meta-analysis of 32 cohorts (1,141,793 participants) found
all-cause mortality **HR 0.94 (0.91–0.98)** and stroke mortality
**HR 0.91 (0.84–0.99)**, with null results for cardiovascular, coronary, and
cancer mortality. A 50 g/day increment gave **HR 0.94 (0.89–0.99)**. The authors
judged certainty low to moderate.

- Zargarzadeh N, Mousavi SM, Santos HO, et al. Legume intake and risk of mortality.
  *Advances in Nutrition* 2023;14(1):64–76.
  PMID: [36811595](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36811595/).

### A daily serving is a reasonable target, not an established requirement

**Strength: weak** for any specific frequency or portion.

Guidance genuinely differs, and the disagreement is legitimate rather than error:
WCRF frames pulses as part of the usual daily diet, Denmark advises ~100 g/day,
and the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet specifies 75 g/day with a stated range of
**0–150 g** — a range that includes zero. Most national guidelines are weekly:
US 1½–3 cup-equivalents/week, AHA ≥2/week, Italy 3/week, Spain ≥4/week.

The Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023 commissioned review found cohort results
null across CVD, CHD, stroke, and type 2 diabetes, with overall certainty
"limited — no conclusion," while RCTs did show risk-factor improvements (total
cholesterol −0.22 mmol/L, LDL −0.19 mmol/L, fasting glucose −0.19 mmol/L). The
authors flag the intake mismatch that explains the split: cohorts observed a median
~20 g/day, trials used >120–150 g/day.

- Thorisdottir B, Arnesen EK, Bärebring L, et al. Legume consumption in adults and
  risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes: a systematic review and
  meta-analysis. *Food & Nutrition Research* 2023;67:9541.
  PMID: [37288088](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37288088/).

State a daily serving as a sensible habit if the user wants a target. Do not
present it as obligatory, and do not attach a disease-prevention promise to it.

### Type 2 diabetes: total legumes show no significant association

**Strength: moderate** (for the null finding).

In 15 cohorts (565,810 people, 32,093 cases), total legume intake gave
**RR 0.95 (0.79–1.14)** — not significant. Only specific soy foods reached
significance: tofu 0.92 (0.84–0.99), soy protein 0.84 (0.75–0.95), soy isoflavones
0.88 (0.81–0.96).

- Tang J, Wan Y, Zhao M, et al. Legume and soy intake and risk of type 2 diabetes.
  *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* 2020;111(3):677–688.
  PMID: [31915830](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31915830/).

"Legumes prevent diabetes" is therefore not supported for legumes in general.
Recommend them for the reasons that do hold — fibre, satiety, protein, glycaemic
quality of the overall meal — see `fiber.md` and `protein.md`.

### Antinutrients exist, and in an adequately cooked varied diet they are not a health concern

**Strength: strong.**

Phytate, raffinose-family oligosaccharides, tannins, lectins, and protease
inhibitors are characterised compounds present in legumes. Phytate forms insoluble
complexes with zinc, iron, calcium, and magnesium in a dose-dependent way. Claiming
these compounds do not exist is not a defensible simplification.

Soaking measurably reduces them. Kidney beans soaked 12 hours: RFO −40–45%, tannins
−23–25%, phytic acid −17–19%, trypsin inhibitors −11%, lectins −6–15%. Successive
soaking and cooking outperform cooking alone.

- Sharma S. A review on traditional technology and safety challenges with regard to
  antinutrients in legume. *Journal of Food Science and Technology*
  2020;58(8):2863–2883.
  DOI: [10.1007/s13197-020-04883-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13197-020-04883-8).

The practical conclusion nevertheless favours legumes: the outcome data above show
regular eaters do as well or better, and mineral-status concerns are amplified by
single-meal study designs relative to a realistic mixed diet. Both halves of this
belong in an answer — the compounds are real, and avoiding legumes over them is not
warranted.

### Soaking and thorough boiling of beans is a food-safety requirement, not a shortcut

**Strength: strong.**

Phytohaemagglutinin (PHA) in *Phaseolus* beans — kidney beans especially — causes
acute toxicity when beans are raw or undercooked. EFSA's 2026 opinion found
sufficient data for risk characterisation only for PHA and states that adequate
processing "such as soaking and boiling beans, effectively deactivates lectins, so
that they pose no health concerns in humans." Critically, it also models the failure
case: where processing deactivates only 50% of lectins, for instance through
insufficient cooking, the margin of exposure falls below the safety threshold,
indicating a health concern.

- EFSA CONTAM Panel. Risks for human health related to the presence of plant lectins
  in food. *EFSA Journal* 2026.
  DOI: [10.2903/j.efsa.2026.9850](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2026.9850).
  Plain-language summary:
  <https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/plain-language-summary/risks-human-health-related-presence-plant-lectins-food>

This is not theoretical. An analysis of 50 suspected UK incidents between 1976 and
1989 identified omitted soaking and cooking at inadequate temperature among the
causes — slow cookers are specifically implicated, because PHA survives prolonged
heating well below boiling.

- Rodhouse JC, Haugh CA, Roberts D, Gilbert RJ. Red kidney bean poisoning in the UK:
  an analysis of 50 suspected incidents between 1976 and 1989. *Epidemiology and
  Infection* 1990;104(3):443–451.
  DOI: [10.1017/S095026880004810X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095026880004810X).

Canned beans are pre-soaked and boiled during processing and require no further
treatment for safety.

Practical points that follow: soak dried kidney beans, then boil vigorously — not
simmer — before any slow cooking. Never treat a slow cooker alone as sufficient for
dried kidney beans. Never advise that soaking can be skipped without loss.

## What to say

1. Recommend legumes as a regular food, weekly-to-daily, without an obligation
   framing or a disease claim.
2. If the user repeats "legumes contain no antinutrients," correct it plainly and
   in both directions: the compounds are real, and they are not a reason to avoid
   legumes.
3. If the user says soaking only speeds up cooking, give the safety reason for
   kidney beans specifically. This is the one point here where being wrong causes
   acute harm.
4. Do not quantify phytate reduction targets or prescribe soaking times as a health
   intervention beyond the safety guidance above.

## Boundary

- Do not present legumes as treatment for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or any
  diagnosed condition.
- Do not advise on legume intake for chronic kidney disease, gout, or FODMAP-related
  conditions without routing per `../../safety-and-context.md`.
- Do not interpret an individual's mineral status or recommend supplementation on
  the basis of phytate intake.
- Cooking instructions in `../../practical/` are practical reference
  material, not medical guidance.
