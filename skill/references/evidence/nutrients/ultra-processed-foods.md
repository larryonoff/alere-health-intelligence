# Ultra-processed foods

Large associations with harm, a contested classification, and a genuine
within-category problem. This entry exists to answer the question honestly in both
directions: the association is real, and "ultra-processed" is not a reliable
verdict on an individual food.

## Claims

### High ultra-processed food intake is associated with many adverse outcomes

**Strength: moderate.**

A 2024 umbrella review of 45 meta-analyses covering nearly 10 million participants
found diets high in ultra-processed foods associated with 32 adverse health
outcomes, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiometabolic disease, several
cancers, gastrointestinal disorders, anxiety, depression, cardiovascular events,
and all-cause mortality.

- Lane MM et al. Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes:
  umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. *BMJ* 2024;384:e077310.
  DOI: [10.1136/bmj-2023-077310](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310).
  PMID: [38418082](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38418082/).
- Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health explainer:
  <https://publichealth.jhu.edu/2025/what-are-ultra-processed-foods>

Labeled `moderate` rather than `strong`: the evidence is overwhelmingly
observational, exposure is measured by food-frequency instruments, and residual
confounding with overall diet quality and socioeconomic factors cannot be excluded
at this scale. Breadth of association is not the same as certainty of causation.

### The strength of association is not uniform within the category

**Strength: moderate**, and this is the most useful thing in the entry.

Within NOVA category 4, evidence separates clearly:

- **Consistent harm**: sugar-sweetened beverages, processed red meat.
- **Null or even inverse associations**: some breads and breakfast cereals.

NOVA classifies all of these identically. That is the classification's central
limitation — it groups by degree of industrial processing, not by nutritional or
health impact.

- Are all ultra-processed foods bad? A critical review of the NOVA classification
  system. *Proc Nutr Soc* 2025.
  PMID: [40757421](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40757421/).
- Science Media Centre expert reaction to the umbrella review:
  <https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-umbrella-review-looking-at-ultra-processed-food-exposure-and-adverse-health-outcomes/>

Practical consequence: **do not tell someone a specific food is harmful because it
is ultra-processed.** Identify what in it actually drives risk — added sugar,
sodium, processed meat, refined starch displacing fibre — or say the evidence for
that particular food is unclear.

### Mechanisms beyond nutrient composition are plausible but unsettled

**Strength: insufficient** for any specific mechanism.

Proposed pathways include energy density and passive overconsumption, food-matrix
disruption affecting satiety, additives and emulsifiers, and packaging contact
materials. A controlled feeding trial found higher energy intake and weight gain on
an ultra-processed diet matched for presented macronutrients, which supports
displacement and overconsumption as contributors.

- Hall KD et al. Ultra-processed diets cause excess calorie intake and weight
  gain: an inpatient randomized controlled trial of ad libitum food intake.
  *Cell Metab* 2019;30(1):67–77.
  DOI: [10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.05.008).
  PMID: [31105044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/).

That trial is small (n=20) and short, but it is randomized and controlled, which is
rare in this literature. Cite it as suggestive of mechanism, not as proof of the
population-level associations.

### Guidelines now name the category explicitly

**Strength: strong** as a statement of what guidelines say.

The DGA 2025–2030 is the first US edition to name highly processed foods
explicitly as a category to avoid. This is one of the areas of genuine agreement
between the guidelines and their academic critics — see
`../guidelines/conflicting-guidance.md`. Critics note the guidance lacks
specificity about which foods and why.

- Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030:
  <https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/>

## Boundary

- Do not use "ultra-processed" as a moral category. `SKILL.md` prohibits clean,
  dirty, and toxic framing, and this is the most common place it appears.
- Convenience and cost are legitimate constraints. Some processed foods
  meaningfully improve dietary adequacy and adherence for people with limited time,
  money, cooking facilities, or physical capacity. Removing them without a
  workable replacement makes eating worse, not better.
- A request to eliminate all processed food, especially as part of a growing
  exclusion list, is a signal — check `../../disordered-eating-safety.md`.
- Fortified foods and medical nutrition products are technically ultra-processed
  and may be necessary. Never discourage a clinically indicated product.

## How to use this entry

Replace the category question with a specific one. "Is this ultra-processed?" is
less useful than "what is in it that matters?" — added sugar, sodium, processed
meat, or refined starch displacing something better.

The practical version of the evidence: reducing sugar-sweetened beverages and
processed meat has the clearest support. Beyond that, the actionable target is
increasing whole and minimally processed foods rather than policing a category.
