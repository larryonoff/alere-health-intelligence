# Dietary supplements

Routing entry. Supplement questions are common, commercially loaded, and the place
where dosing prohibitions are most likely to be tested.

## Where the authority is

**NIH Office of Dietary Supplements** is the primary source: ingredient, dose,
interaction, and safety information, maintained by NIH, available
programmatically.

- Fact sheets: <https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/list-all/>
- API: <https://ods.od.nih.gov/api/>
- Label database: <https://dsld.od.nih.gov/>
- PubMed Dietary Supplement Subset:
  <https://ods.od.nih.gov/Research/PubMed_Dietary_Supplement_Subset.aspx>

Prefer ODS over any commercial, advocacy, or seller-affiliated source. For European
tolerable upper intake levels, use EFSA:
<https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/food-supplements>

For longevity-marketed compounds, use `../longevity/unproven-interventions.md`.

## Claims

### Routine multivitamin use does not prevent disease in healthy adults

**Strength: moderate.**

The US Preventive Services Task Force found insufficient evidence to recommend
multivitamin or single-nutrient supplements for cardiovascular disease or cancer
prevention in this population, and recommended against beta-carotene and vitamin E.

- US Preventive Services Task Force. Vitamin, mineral, and multivitamin
  supplementation to prevent cardiovascular disease and cancer: US Preventive
  Services Task Force recommendation statement. *JAMA* 2022;327(23):2326–2333.
  DOI: [10.1001/jama.2022.8970](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970).
  PMID: [35727271](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35727271/).

This applies to prevention in adequately nourished people. It does not apply to
diagnosed deficiency, malabsorption, pregnancy, restrictive diets, or clinically
indicated supplementation.

### Some supplementation is genuinely indicated

**Strength: strong** for specific situations, established by guidelines rather than
by this entry.

Examples where supplementation is standard practice: folic acid before and in early
pregnancy; vitamin B12 on a vegan diet; vitamin D where deficiency is documented or
exposure is inadequate; iron in diagnosed deficiency; specific nutrients after
bariatric surgery or with malabsorption.

Each of these is a clinical determination requiring an individual assessment. State
that supplementation exists and is appropriate in that situation, and route.

### More is not better, and upper limits exist

**Strength: strong.**

Tolerable upper intake levels are set for many nutrients because excess causes
harm — iron, vitamin A, vitamin D, selenium, and niacin among others. Fat-soluble
vitamins accumulate. High-dose single nutrients have caused harm in trials,
including the beta-carotene and vitamin E findings above.

- EFSA tolerable upper intake levels:
  <https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/dietary-reference-values>

### Supplements interact with medication

**Strength: strong** as a general fact; specifics are per-pair.

Well-documented examples: St John's wort induces drug metabolism and reduces the
effect of many medications; vitamin K affects warfarin; calcium, iron, and soy
affect levothyroxine absorption; high-dose fish oil and anticoagulants.

Check ODS for the specific pair. Do not reason about an interaction from mechanism.

### Regulation differs from medicines

**Strength: strong.**

In the US, supplements are not approved for safety and efficacy before marketing.
Content can differ from label. Third-party verification programs exist but are
voluntary. In the EU, food supplements are regulated as food, not medicine.

Practical consequence: product claims are not evidence, and a product's presence on
a shelf implies nothing about efficacy.

## Boundary

**This entry never produces a dose.** Not in IU, micrograms, milligrams, or "one
capsule daily" — regardless of how benign the compound, how common the dose, or how
directly the user asks. Dosing is a clinical act, prohibited by
`../../safety-and-context.md`.

Also never:

- interpret a blood level or a laboratory result;
- set a target blood level;
- recommend a specific brand or product;
- advise on stopping or replacing a prescribed medication with a supplement;
- design a supplement stack or protocol.

Route to a clinician or pharmacist for: any dose question, any interaction with a
current medication, pregnancy or breastfeeding, children, chronic kidney or liver
disease, and any documented deficiency.

## How to answer

1. Say what the compound is and what it is claimed to do.
2. Give the actual state of evidence, at its real strength.
3. Name the population it applies to — prevention in healthy adults is a different
   question from correcting a deficiency.
4. Point to ODS as a checkable source the person can read themselves.
5. Name what requires a clinician: the dose, the interaction, the diagnosis.
6. Note cost and opportunity cost where a food-based alternative exists.

The pattern that works is the same as for longevity compounds: distinguish "changes
a measurable value" from "improves health," and redirect toward what has evidence
rather than only declining.
