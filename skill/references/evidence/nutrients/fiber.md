# Dietary fibre

The most consistently underconsumed component of the diet in high-income
countries, with unusually strong evidence across multiple outcomes — and far less
public attention than protein.

## Claims

### 25–29 g per day is associated with substantially lower disease and mortality

**Strength: moderate.** This matches the certainty the authors themselves assign:
the series grades the evidence for dietary fibre as *moderate* for critical
outcomes. A source's own GRADE rating governs the label — do not upgrade it
because the effect size is large or the series is prominent.

A Lancet series of systematic reviews and meta-analyses covering nearly 40 years
of observational studies and clinical trials found risk reduction across critical
outcomes was **greatest at 25–29 g per day**, with:

- observational data suggesting a **15–30% decrease** in all-cause and
  cardiovascular mortality and in the incidence of coronary heart disease, stroke,
  type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer;
- clinical trials showing significantly lower body weight, systolic blood
  pressure, and total cholesterol at higher versus lower intakes.

Benefit continued to increase above 29 g/day, with no clear threshold identified.

- Reynolds A et al. Carbohydrate quality and human health: a series of systematic
  reviews and meta-analyses. *Lancet* 2019;393(10170):434–445.
  DOI: [10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9).
  PMID: [30638909](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/).

An updated 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohorts
confirmed the inverse association with all-cause and cause-specific mortality.

- Dietary fibre intake and all-cause and cause-specific mortality: updated
  systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies.
  *Clin Nutr* 2023.
  <https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614(23)00363-1/abstract>

### Fibre and whole grains improve diabetes management

**Strength: strong.**

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses in diabetes populations found higher fibre
intake improved glycaemic control and cardiometabolic risk markers.

- Reynolds AN et al. Dietary fibre in hypertension and cardiovascular disease
  management, and dietary fibre and whole grains in diabetes management:
  systematic review and meta-analyses. *PLoS Med* 2020;17(3):e1003053.
  DOI: [10.1371/journal.pmed.1003053](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003053).
  PMID: [32142510](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32142510/).

Directly relevant to the metabolic cluster — see `../conditions/prediabetes.md`
and `../conditions/type-2-diabetes.md`.

### Viscous soluble fibre specifically lowers LDL

**Strength: strong.**

One of the four components of the Portfolio dietary pattern. See
`../conditions/elevated-ldl.md` for the LDL evidence and magnitudes.

- Chiavaroli L et al. Portfolio Dietary Pattern and Cardiovascular Disease.
  *Prog Cardiovasc Dis* 2018.
  DOI: [10.1016/j.pcad.2018.05.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pcad.2018.05.004).

### Reference intakes

**Strength: strong** as reference values.

EFSA sets an adequate intake of 25 g/day for adults; NNR 2023 and most national
bodies recommend in the range of 25–35 g/day. Typical intakes in high-income
countries fall well below these.

- EFSA dietary reference values:
  <https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/dietary-reference-values>
- Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023: <https://pub.norden.org/nord2023-003/>

These are population reference points, not individual prescriptions.

### Evidence for fibre-rich foods is broader than for isolated supplements

**Strength: moderate.**

The evidence base is built on fibre-rich foods — whole grains, legumes,
vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds — which deliver fibre types, micronutrients and
food-matrix effects together. Isolated fibre supplements have narrower evidence,
mostly for specific endpoints such as LDL or stool consistency, and do not
reproduce the whole-diet findings.

- Reynolds A et al. Carbohydrate quality and human health. *Lancet* 2019.
  DOI: [10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9).

## Boundary

- Increase gradually and with adequate fluid; rapid large increases commonly cause
  bloating and discomfort, which is the main reason people abandon the change.
- **Do not recommend increasing fibre without checking for**: inflammatory bowel
  disease in an active flare, known stricture, recent bowel surgery,
  gastroparesis, or a clinician-prescribed low-fibre or low-residue diet. In these
  situations more fibre can be harmful.
- IBS is a special case. Fibre type matters, some fermentable fibres worsen
  symptoms, and low-FODMAP approaches conflict with generic "eat more fibre"
  advice. Route rather than generalize.
- Fibre supplements are a supplement question — route per
  `../../safety-and-context.md`.

## How to use this entry

This is often the highest-yield single change available, and the least-discussed.
The typical gap between actual and recommended intake is large, the evidence spans
several hard outcomes, and one concrete swap — legumes added to one meal, whole
grain in place of refined — is a feasible next action.

When someone is focused on protein, fibre is frequently the actual gap. Say so
without dismissing their question.
