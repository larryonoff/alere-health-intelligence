# When a qualified clinician makes an unsourced claim

Read this when a user attributes a claim to a doctor, a course, a documentary, or
a health influencer, and the support offered is the speaker's qualification
rather than a traceable source.

This is not the case covered by `conflicting-guidance.md`. There, two bodies of
comparable standing disagree and both can be cited. Here there is nothing to
cite: the claim appeals to evidence in the abstract — "there are studies,"
"official medical statistics," "scientists compared" — without naming any
document that can be opened and checked.

### An unsourced claim from a credentialed speaker

**Strength: insufficient.** Not because the claim is necessarily false, but
because nothing has been offered that could establish it. Absence of a source is
information, and it is reported as such rather than as refutation.

The standards that define what a traceable claim looks like are external and
checkable:

- ICMJE recommendations on authorship, conflict-of-interest disclosure, and
  source attribution: <https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/>
- EQUATOR Network reporting guidelines, including CONSORT for trials and PRISMA
  for systematic reviews: <https://www.equator-network.org/>
- Cochrane Handbook on risk of bias and why uncontrolled clinical observation
  does not establish effect: <https://training.cochrane.org/handbook>

These are cited here for the standard, not for any nutrition claim.

## Why this file exists

An unsourced claim from an anonymous blog is easy to handle. The hard case is a
licensed physician with genuine clinical experience, real hospital affiliations,
and verifiable training under named researchers. The credential is real. The
citation is still missing, and those are different things.

Deferring to the credential and dismissing the person are both failures. The
first adopts unverified claims; the second is unfair, usually wrong, and drives
the user away from a conversation they came to have honestly.

## Mechanisms observed

Each of these substitutes something for a citation. Naming the mechanism is more
useful than restating the rule.

- **Credential in place of citation.** "I am a physician, ten years in leading
  clinics, I perform angiographies myself." Qualification raises the prior that a
  claim is defensible; it does not evidence any specific claim. Distinct from
  borrowed credibility in `../../../SKILL.md`, where a citation is stretched —
  here personal expertise occupies the slot the citation should fill.
- **Mentorship as provenance.** "I trained under Barnard and Esselstyn." Real
  people, real clinics, no source for the claim at hand. Those researchers'
  published work can be cited for what it actually reports.
- **Tradition synthesis labelled evidence-based.** Combining evidence-based
  medicine with naturopathy, Ayurveda, and traditional Chinese medicine, and
  presenting the result under the first label. Every claim still needs its own
  source.
- **Mechanism in place of outcome.** Tight junctions, permeability, "toxins
  entering the blood" — plausible physiology with no clinical endpoint. Adjacent
  to surrogate-reported-as-outcome, but weaker: a surrogate is at least measured.
- **Absolute safety as a selling point.** "No contraindications at all,"
  "physically impossible to overdose." An asserted absence of harm is itself a
  reason for caution, not reassurance.
- **Self-declared non-evidence, recommendation retained.** "This isn't
  scientific, but it genuinely helps, I see it in my patients." The
  acknowledgement is the operative fact; uncontrolled observation does not
  establish effect.
- **Internal contradiction across a body of material.** Figures and principles
  that conflict between sessions of the same course indicate the material is not
  reconciled against sources, independent of any single claim's truth.

## How to answer

**Check the safety files first.** If the quoted claim involves restriction,
elimination, weight loss, calorie targets, fasting, paediatrics, medication, or a
diagnosed condition, then `../../disordered-eating-safety.md` and
`../../safety-and-context.md` govern the *shape* of the reply and this file governs
only how to handle the provenance. In that case skip step 1: do not produce the
substantive nutrition answer those files withhold. Naming the missing source is
still correct and never a substitute for the safety response.

Step 1 below applies only when no safety file fires.

1. **Answer the underlying question from the reviewed corpus** at the corpus
   strength label, citing the underlying source. This is the substance of the
   reply; the provenance discussion is secondary.
2. **Separate credibility from verification, once, without insult.** The
   clinician may well be right; what is missing is a source that shows it. Do not
   speculate about motive, competence, or commercial interest.
3. **Name what would settle it** — the specific guideline, systematic review, or
   trial that would need to exist. This is more useful than a verdict, and it
   respects a user who is trying to evaluate what they were told.
4. **Do not treat agreement as verification.** When the claim matches the corpus,
   say the corpus supports it and cite that. The course is not the source, and a
   correct claim from an unreliable source stays correct — do not doubt a
   well-supported statement because of who repeated it.
5. **Where the claim is safety-critical, the safety file governs**, not this one.
   Route per `../../safety-and-context.md` and
   `../../disordered-eating-safety.md`.
6. **Never mirror the material's register.** Reversal and cure claims, promises
   about medication, and disease-root-cause framing are out of scope regardless
   of who stated them.

## Boundary

- Do not cite a course, video, documentary, or book as the source for a health
  claim. Find the underlying literature or say no reliable source covers it.
- Do not use "a doctor said it" as a strength label of any kind.
- Do not disparage the named person, and do not adopt conspiracy framing in
  either direction.
- A user quoting harmful advice is not endorsing it. Answer the question rather
  than correcting the user.
